Tumor Markers: What These Blood Tests Actually Tell Doctors — and What They Don't

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Tumor marker tests measure specific proteins in the blood that can rise with certain cancers. Doctors use them to monitor treatment response, track recurrence, and guide care—but tumor markers alone cannot confirm a cancer diagnosis or pinpoint a tumor's location.

Accurate interpretation depends on several factors: trends across multiple tests rather than a single value, lab-to-lab variability, benign conditions that can elevate markers, and the real possibility of false positives or false negatives. Context matters as much as the number itself.

Explanation

Blood tests for tumor markers have become a routine part of cancer care and monitoring. Yet many people wonder: What exactly do these tests show, and what's beyond their reach? Below, we'll break down the basics of a tumor markers blood test, explain how doctors use the results—and where you should draw the line when interpreting them.

What Are Tumor Markers?

Definition: Tumor markers are substances—usually proteins—found in blood, urine or tissues. They can be produced by cancer cells or by your body in response to cancer.

What Tumor Markers Tell Doctors

Tumor markers are most valuable when used in context. Here's how physicians typically apply them:

Monitoring treatment response• A falling marker level during chemotherapy or targeted therapy often indicates the treatment is working.• Stable or rising levels may prompt a change in treatment plan.

Detecting recurrence• After successful treatment, regular marker checks can catch early signs of a cancer coming back.• An unexpected rise in marker levels might lead to imaging studies or further tests.

Assessing tumor burden• Higher levels often correlate with larger tumors or more widespread disease.• Tracking trends over time gives doctors a sense of how quickly a tumor is growing or shrinking.

What Tumor Markers Don't Tell Doctors

It's crucial to understand the limitations of a tumor markers blood test:

Not a standalone diagnostic tool• Elevated markers alone can't confirm cancer. Many benign conditions (inflammation, infections, noncancerous growths) can raise levels.• Normal marker levels don't guarantee you're cancer-free—some tumors don't produce detectable markers.

No precise location or type• Tumor markers rarely pinpoint where a tumor is located. Imaging (CT, MRI, ultrasound) and biopsies remain essential.• Multiple cancers can share the same marker (e.g., CEA can rise in colorectal, pancreatic, breast and lung cancers).

Risk of false positives and negatives• False positives may lead to unnecessary anxiety, invasive tests or treatments.• False negatives can provide false reassurance, delaying diagnosis if symptoms persist.

When Doctors Order Tumor Marker Tests

Screening: Rare, except for PSA in men with risk factors or family history of prostate cancer.

Diagnosis support: When imaging or symptoms suggest cancer, marker tests may add information.

Monitoring: During and after treatment, to gauge effectiveness and detect early recurrence.

Prognosis estimation: In some cancers, very high levels at diagnosis can predict aggressive behavior.

Interpreting Tumor Marker Levels

Trend matters more than a single value• A gradual decline or rise over multiple tests is more meaningful than one isolated result.• Sudden spikes should prompt further investigation rather than immediate alarm.

Percent change vs. absolute number• A small increase in a very high marker level can be less significant than a large percentage jump from a low baseline.

Factors Affecting Tumor Marker Levels

Noncancerous health issues• Liver disease, kidney dysfunction or inflammatory conditions can alter marker readings.

Medications and treatments• Certain drugs, surgeries or radiation can temporarily raise or lower levels.

Laboratory variability• Different labs use different equipment and reference ranges; always compare your results to the same lab when possible.
