If you experience any of the common signs and symptoms of heart disease, such as chest pain, persistent breathlessness, ongoing fatigue and weakness, or an irregular, fluttering, or racing heartbeat, it’s likely you’ll need an EKG. To know what to expect, here’s a breakdown of how the tests are conducted and what the results can mean.

What Is Involved With an EKG Test and Why Do I Have to Take It?

As the Mayo Clinic describes it, an EKG is conducted via 12 small plastic sensors (electrodes). They have adhesive on one side that allows them to stick to the skin. Some men may need to have their chest hair shaved so the electrodes can be attached securely.

Through the electrodes, electrical impulses that make the heart beat are recorded by a computer. With each beat, an electrical impulse (or “wave”) causes the muscle to pump blood from the heart. The waves are displayed on a monitor or paper, and medical professionals who know how to read the information interpret whether the contractions in the upper and lower chambers of the heart are normal.

As the American Heart Association (AHA) describes it, the right and left atria (the upper chambers, or ventricles) make a wave called a “P wave,” the bottom right and left chambers make a wave called a “QRS complex,” and the return to a resting state for the ventricles is recorded as a “T wave.”

The EKG gives two types of information, according to the AHA. First, it records how long it takes a wave to travel from one part of the heart to the next, showing if electrical activity is normal, slow, fast, or irregular. Second, the amount of electrical activity passing through the heart muscle can reveal if parts of the heart are damaged, too large, or are overworked.

Typically, an EKG is performed in a healthcare provider’s office, a clinic, or a hospital room. Recording the information takes only about 5 to 10 minutes as the patient lies faceup (supine) on a special bed or table. Results are instantaneous.

“The doctor is going to see EKG readings immediately, so if there’s something there that’s worrisome, they are going to act sooner than later,” says Dr. Bauman.

What Are Signs That I May Need an EKG?

A doctor may recommend an EKG if you have chest pain, light-headedness, dizziness, confusion, heart palpitations, rapid pulse, shortness of breath, weakness, fatigue, or a decline in the ability to exercise.

“Sometimes when a person has had a stroke and we don't know the reason for it, that person would most likely have an initial evaluation with an electrocardiogram to see if they have atrial fibrillation or some other rhythm abnormality that could have caused the stroke,” explains Bauman.

Johns Hopkins University says that an “ambulatory EKG” may be recommended in cases where people experience symptoms such as dizziness, fainting, low blood pressure, ongoing tiredness, and palpitations but a standard EKG doesn't show a clear cause of these problems. With an ambulatory EKG, patients wear a Holter monitor. This battery-operated portable device measures and records your heart’s activity continuously for one to two days as you go about your everyday routine.

If symptoms are still unexplained or difficult to detect, a person may be given a cardiac event monitor — a device often worn for a month or more or implanted. While the Holter monitor records continuously — usually for 24 to 48 hours, the event monitor records only when you activate it, according to Johns Hopkins Medicine. What Do Abnormal Results Mean?

Abnormal EKG results don’t always signal a major heart health problem. Although a person can have a variation in the normal rhythm without it affecting their overall health, unusual EKG results can be a red flag for certain health issues, such as:

Heart attack With this type of cardiac event, blood flow is disrupted, which can cause heart tissue to die and prevent electrical impulses from working as they should. An EKG can show that a heart attack is currently happening or if a person may have had a heart attack in the past that went unrecognized. Finding this out can pinpoint heart risk and help inform people that they need to take steps toward improving their heart health.

Defects or irregularities The heart may have a shape or size that makes it work harder to pump blood, and this strain can show up on an EKG.

Medication side effects Drugs meant to help the heart can sometimes cause heart troubles. Some medications — such as beta-blockers and calcium channel blockers for high blood pressure and sodium channel blockers for irregular heartbeat — can disrupt heart rates and rhythms, and these disturbances can be detected by an EKG.

Electrolyte imbalance When minerals that are vital to bodily functions (electrolytes) are in imbalance, electrical impulses can be out of whack. Electrolytes include potassium, sodium, calcium, or magnesium. Minor imbalances may be corrected through diet, but extreme cases may require intravenous fluids and medications.

Arrhythmia (irregular heartbeat) An EKG can reveal if the heart is beating out of rhythm, or too fast or slow.

What Can I Expect Next?

Following an EKG, a doctor may recommend treatments right away. Depending on the condition detected, a person may need to get on a specific medication or have a medical procedure.

For example, a person whose EKG suggests they had a prior heart attack may require more testing, like a cardiac ultrasound, to look at heart function, or another study to evaluate blood flow to the heart.

Bauman warns that while an EKG can provide information about heart function, it shouldn’t be an ultimate gauge for heart health. She stresses that even though an EKG is a reliable tool for detecting electrical abnormalities in your heart rhythm, it may not detect every type of heart trouble.

Other diagnostic tools need to be used to fully measure hearth health, such as blood tests and other cardiac imaging. So even those with normal EKG readings should follow lifestyle habits to maintain a healthy heart, such as eating a healthy diet, exercising, and not smoking.

“You can have a perfectly normal electrocardiogram, and the next day have a heart attack,” she says. “So you don't want the false reassurance.”
