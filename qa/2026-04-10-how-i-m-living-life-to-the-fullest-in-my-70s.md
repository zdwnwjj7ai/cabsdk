# How I'm Living Life to the Fullest in My 70s

JEREMIAH HOLLIS JR. knows a thing or two about overcoming challenges to keep living the good life. After undergoing two knee replacements, a hip replacement, and triple bypass surgery over the past 10 years, the 71-year-old Air Force veteran is staying active every day, relishing time with family and friends, and doing his part to serve his community.

A Perspective Shift

“NOBODY TEACHES US how to grow old. We have to learn, so I’m learning as I go,” says Hollis, who worked as an engineer in the auto industry and for the federal and his local government before retiring. With no instruction manual to guide him, he sought lessons from those he loved and admired—like his father, Jeremiah Sr., who also spent his career in the auto industry, working tirelessly for 54 years.

“People reinvent themselves all the time. Life doesn’t end after a certain age—it can just be beginning for some,” says Isabel Souffront, MD, a Florida-based physician with Conviva Senior Primary Care.

“Pickleball and similar activities challenge hand-eye coordination. That’s brain exercise, too.”

When Jeremiah Sr. passed away just two years after retiring, Hollis had a lightbulb moment, resolving to spend his older adulthood living life to the fullest. “One of the last things my father said was, ‘Don’t do it like I did. Don’t be a workaholic.’ He didn't have time to enjoy his retirement,” Hollis says. “I saw how it destroyed him, not being able to do things and enjoy life. So I decided that I’m not going to do that.”

Purpose in Pickleball

SINCE THEN, Hollis has been on a mission to stay strong both physically and mentally. A lifelong athlete, he devoted much of his free time in his 60s to playing golf, riding his bike, and taking walks with his wife. “Living fully doesn’t mean kicking back in an easy chair,” Hollis says. “I’ll have plenty of time to rest when I’m lying in that box.”

But then he had a series of health problems. Decades of wear and tear had taken their toll on his body, and he needed both of his knees and one of his hips replaced. Within the same stretch of time, he had triple bypass heart surgery. Refusing to be defeated, Hollis saw these as short-term setbacks, not game-enders, and went all in on physical therapy to get his strength back.

When his doctors gave him the all-clear to return to higher-intensity activity, Hollis took up a new sport. Already an avid golfer—a 4 handicap with six holes-in-one under his belt—he went with one of his golf buddies for a drop-in pickleball game, and was quickly hooked. And he discovered he had a knack for it, having played other racket sports like badminton, tennis, and squash when he was younger. “It felt like a natural progression,” he says. “The acceleration, the coordination, the precision shots—it all drew me in. It uses a lot of energy.”

That first game turned out to be the start of an impressive run. To date, Hollis has now captured 16 pickleball medals and participated in two national competitions—including the National Senior Games presented by Humana, a biennial multisport event for more than 12,000 athletes ages 50 and up.

Not Just Physical

PICKLEBALL OFFERS HOLLIS more than just a chance to flex his competitive muscles. The fast-paced game delivers a serious workout, which helps maintain his heart health while protecting his brain from the potential effects of aging. “Keeping yourself balanced, focusing on the ball—you’re moving and increasing oxygen throughout your body and strengthening your muscles,” says Dr. Souffront. “Pickleball and similar activities challenge hand-eye coordination. That’s brain exercise, too.”

Just as important, hitting up regular games in his community has become one of Hollis’s favorite ways to build meaningful social ties. That camaraderie doesn’t just feel good—he credits it with keeping his brain sharp. “You get the icing on the cake with the socialization of it all. I’ve made a lot of new friends,” says Hollis, laughing over the multiple group chats he has going about upcoming pickleball games.

“You’ve got to exercise your brain. It’s like anything in life.”

Exercising and socializing are two key ingredients for healthy cognitive aging, Dr. Souffront points out. “When you’re physically active within your community, you’re getting out and interacting with others, so it goes hand in hand. One of the best ways to avoid social isolation is to stay active.”

That doesn’t have to mean playing in pickleball competitions, of course. “Pick any sport or activity and take baby steps. Walk a little further each day,” says Hollis, who takes inspiration from his fellow veterans. “Those guys were wounded in the Vietnam War, and they’re still competing.”

A Healthy Balance

YOU CAN’T BLAME Hollis for taking a rest every so often. But in keeping with his soak-it-all-up philosophy, when he’s not working his body, he engages in other satisfying activities that give his mind a workout. These include picking up volunteer shifts with charitable causes and local veterans organizations.

“You’ve got to exercise your brain. It’s like anything in life,” says Dr. Souffront. “If you can strengthen that muscle, more power to you. It will keep you sharp.” Family time also serves as a way to stay engaged. Hollis and his wife host monthly game nights with their six children and three grandchildren, where the whole gang plays board games or tackles a challenging puzzle together. “It’s a time to relax, have fun, and share legacy moments,” he says.

Looking Forward

HOLLIS MOST RECENTLY participated in the National Veterans Golden Age Games in Memphis, Tennessee, and looks forward to competing in this year’s National Senior Olympics this summer in Des Moines, where he aims to earn another pickleball medal. He’ll also be recognized as a Humana Game Changer—one of 10 National Senior Games athletes selected because of their passion for continuous growth, both physically and mentally.

Even if you’re not an athlete heading to the event, you can join the competition online with Humana Cognitive GamesTM. Running from July 14 to August 11, this series of virtual games and puzzles is a fun, interactive way to exercise your mind—and research shows that older adults who participate in brain-training sessions can experience long-term cognitive improvements.

“Playing stimulating games can reduce the chance of brain fog,” says Dr. Souffront. “You’re challenging yourself, you’re stimulating your brain—and that helps protect against mental decline.” Hopefully, the challenge will inspire you to get into the habit of training your brain for a few minutes each day. Because, as with every other muscle in your body, the more you work it, the stronger and more resilient it becomes.
