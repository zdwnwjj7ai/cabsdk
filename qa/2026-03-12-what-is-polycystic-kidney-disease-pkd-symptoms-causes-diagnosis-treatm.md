# What Is Polycystic Kidney Disease (PKD)? Symptoms, Causes, Diagnosis, Treatment, and Prevention

What Is Polycystic Kidney Disease? Symptoms, Causes, Diagnosis, Treatment, and Prevention

Polycystic kidney disease (PKD) is a genetic disorder marked by the growth of many fluid-filled sacs (cysts) in your kidneys. These cysts can grow to replace normal kidney tissue over time, leading to reduced kidney function and, potentially, kidney failure.

About 600,000 people are believed to have PKD in the United States, according to the National Kidney Foundation. The condition is responsible for about 5 percent of cases of kidney failure, making it the fourth leading cause of kidney failure.

Signs and symptoms of PKD — such as high blood pressure, headaches, or localized abdominal pain — typically develop in adulthood, although a rarer form of the condition tends to cause symptoms in early infancy. Your doctor will diagnose PKD based on your symptoms, family history, and imaging tests such as an ultrasound. Genetic tests are usually reserved for special situations, such as unclear imaging results.

There is no cure for PKD, but treatment and lifestyle behaviors can slow the growth of cysts and progression toward kidney failure.

Signs and Symptoms of Polycystic Kidney Disease

Even though people are born with the genetic variant that causes polycystic kidney disease, most of them don’t develop signs or symptoms of the disorder until they’re 30 to 40 years old, the National Kidney Foundation notes. That’s because smaller cysts in your kidneys often cause no signs or symptoms. Often the first sign of PKD is high blood pressure, which may be detected at a regular doctor’s appointment.

PKD can also cause the following symptoms, according to the Mayo Clinic:

Pain in the side or back of your abdomen

Larger abdominal size

Sense of fullness in your abdomen

Blood in urine

Recurrent bladder or kidney infections

Fluttering or pounding heartbeat

Fluttering or pounding in your chest is caused by what’s known as a floppy heart valve, which affects about 25 percent of people with PKD, according to the National Kidney Foundation.

A less common form of PKD (known as autosomal recessive PKD) typically causes symptoms beginning shortly after birth, although sometimes the symptoms don’t appear until later in childhood, the Mayo Clinic notes.

Causes and Risk Factors of Polycystic Kidney Disease

Polycystic kidney disease is an inherited genetic disorder, meaning it’s passed from parents to their children. There are two main forms of PKD, which are classified based on how the genetic variant that causes the condition is passed on:

Autosomal dominant PKD

Autosomal recessive PKD

Autosomal dominant PKD is by far the more common form of PKD, accounting for about 90 percent of all cases, according to the National Kidney Fund. If one parent has this form of PKD, there is a 50 percent chance that each child will inherit the condition.

Autosomal recessive PKD is much less common and tends to cause symptoms that begin in early infancy. For this form of PKD to be passed on to a child, both parents need to have the genetic variant for it, although the parents won’t necessarily have PKD. If both parents have the genetic variant, there is a 25 percent chance that each child will have this form of PKD, the Mayo Clinic notes.

Autosomal dominant PKD is seen in one in every 500 to 1,000 people, while autosomal recessive PKD is believed to affect one in every 20,000 to 40,000 people, according to the National Library of Medicine.

Since PKD is an inherited disorder, you’re either born with it or you’re not — there’s nothing you can do to change your likelihood of having it. But certain lifestyle behaviors can slow the growth of cysts in people with the condition, so you may want to get screened for PKD — before you develop any symptoms — if you have a parent, sibling, or child with the condition. Talk to your doctor about this option if you’re interested in getting screened.

How Is Polycystic Kidney Disease Diagnosed?

Polycystic kidney disease is typically diagnosed based on symptoms, health history and family history, a physical exam, and imaging tests, according to the National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK).

If your doctor suspects PKD based on your symptoms and any family history of the disease, it’s likely that you’ll undergo one or more imaging tests to look at your kidneys. These tests may include the following:

Ultrasound This technique uses sound waves to create an image of internal structures such as your kidneys, and it is often the first recommended imaging test for PKD.

Computed Tomography This technique uses a series of X-rays and a computer to create detailed images of your urinary tract, and it may involve the injection of a dye known as a contrast medium.

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) This technique uses radio waves and magnets to create detailed images of internal organs and soft tissues, and it may also involve injecting a contrast medium. MRI is often used to measure the size and growth of cysts and kidneys in people with PKD, as part of monitoring the progression of the condition.

Less commonly, PKD can be diagnosed based on a genetic test. This may happen if your imaging test results are inconclusive, or if you have a parent, sibling, or child with PKD and want to known if you have the condition even though you haven’t developed any signs or symptoms.

Genetic testing for PKD uses a small blood or saliva sample that is sent away to a lab. This form of testing is costly and fails to detect PKD in about 15 percent of people who have the condition, which is why it’s not a standard form of testing, the National Kidney Foundation notes.

You may want to undergo genetic testing for PKD if you have a family history of the condition and are considering donating a kidney, or if you’re considering starting a family. A genetic counselor can help you decide whether to have this test, and help you understand the results and their potential implications, NIDDK notes.

Treatment of Polycystic Kidney Disease

The goal of treatment for PKD is to slow the growth of cysts in your kidneys, thus preventing or delaying kidney failure. Lifestyle behaviors also play a key role in managing PKD.

Depending on how severe your PKD is, how quickly it’s progressing, and other considerations, treatment may involve the following approaches, according to the Cleveland Clinic:

Blood pressure management

Since 2018, a drug called tolvaptan (Jynarque) has been approved in the United States to slow the decline of kidney function in adults with the most common form of PKD (autosomal dominant PKD). Tolvaptan is taken by mouth as a pill. This treatment carries certain risks, including serious liver problems, the Mayo Clinic notes. Talk to your doctor about whether tolvaptan is right for you.

Many people with PKD have high blood pressure due to reduced kidney function. Blood pressure management is a key element of controlling PKD, and it can help prevent heart disease and stroke as well as further damage to your kidneys. Blood pressure can be managed through dietary changes, exercise, and certain medications.

PKD can cause pain via bursting cysts, infections, or kidney stones. Depending on the cause of your pain, your doctor may prescribe or recommend a pain medication. Be sure to consult your doctor before taking any over-the-counter pain medications, since some of these drugs can contribute to kidney damage.

If your kidney function declines to the point of kidney failure, you’ll need to begin dialysis — a procedure to filter waste products and excess fluid from your blood, since your kidneys can no longer do this. Different methods of dialysis are available, and you may have the option of undergoing the procedure regularly either at home or at a dialysis center.

As an alternative to dialysis or after beginning dialysis, you may be a candidate for a kidney transplant. A kidney transplant can lead to improved quality of life, but it also carries certain risks, so it’s not recommended for everyone with kidney failure.

Lifestyle Modifications

A generally healthy lifestyle is particularly important for people with PKD, since this can help support kidney health. That means not smoking, eating healthy foods and limiting salt in your diet, getting enough exercise, and controlling your body weight, the National Kidney Foundation notes.

There are also a couple of lifestyle recommendations specific to people with PKD:

Drinking lots of water throughout the day

Avoiding caffeinated beverages

Drinking lots of water may slow the growth of cysts in PKD, while caffeinated beverages can contribute to the growth of cysts, according to the National Kidney Foundation.

While there is no specific diet that’s recommended for PKD, limiting your salt intake is a key element of blood pressure control, which is important for people with PKD. A diet that limits fat and calories may also help you control your body weight, which is important for overall health in people with PKD.

While exercise is important, people with PKD should avoid contact sports that may cause impact-related kidney injuries. You should also take care not to become dehydrated when doing any kind of physical activity.

Treating PKD in Infants

For infants with autosomal recessive PKD, treatments are designed to address the often severe health effects that occur early on. Those treatments may include:

Breathing Support If your child has breathing problems due to underdeveloped lungs, mechanical ventilation may be needed.

Growth Therapy Underweight or underdeveloped infants may need treatments like nutrition therapy or human growth hormone.

Blood Pressure Control Medications may be used to help control blood pressure and help delay kidney failure.

Kidney Removal If kidney enlargement due to PKD makes breathing impossible, one or both kidneys may need to be removed, according to NIDDK. Removing both kidneys means that your child will need ongoing dialysis or a kidney transplant.

Kidney or Liver Transplant A kidney transplant may be recommended in some cases of severe PKD, and since autosomal recessive PKD can cause serious liver problems, sometimes a combined kidney and liver transplant is recommended.

Complications of Polycystic Kidney Disease

Polycystic kidney disease can lead to a range of health complications — including, but not limited to, problems related to reduced kidney function. In fact, cysts similar to those in the kidneys of people with PKD can grow in other organs, potentially causing pain or dysfunction when they bulge or rupture, the National Kidney Foundation notes.

People with PKD are at higher risk of the following health problems:

High blood pressure

Kidney failure

Brain aneurysm

Heart valve problems

Liver cysts

Diverticulosis (pouches or sacs in the wall of your colon)

Pregnancy complications

While most pregnancies in women with PKD are successful and uncomplicated, PKD puts you at higher risk for a life-threatening disorder called preeclampsia, which is marked by very high blood pressure. You’re at higher risk for preeclampsia if you have high blood pressure or declining kidney function before you become pregnant, the Mayo Clinic notes.

About 1 in 4 people with PKD develop a heart valve problem known as mitral valve prolapse, which means one of your heart valves doesn’t close properly and leads to blood leaking backward.

Loss of kidney function is probably the most serious complication linked to PKD, and it affects nearly everyone with PKD at some level. About half of people with PKD will have kidney failure by age 60, and about 60 percent will have kidney failure by age 70, according to the National Kidney Foundation.

Prevention of Polycystic Kidney Disease

Since polycystic kidney disease is an inherited genetic disorder, you’re either born with the condition or you’re not — even though it typically takes decades to develop symptoms of the most common form of PKD. But it may be possible to delay or slow the development of cysts in your kidneys through lifestyle measures, such as drinking lots of water and avoiding caffeinated beverages.

If PKD runs in your family, you may consider getting a genetic test to find out if you have the condition even before you develop any symptoms. A genetic counselor can discuss the reasons why you might or might not choose to do this — but some people are interested in a genetic test so that they can take precautions to try to slow the development of PKD if they have it.

You may also be interested in genetic testing if you’re considering starting a family, to know what the risk is that you’ll pass PKD to any potential child.
