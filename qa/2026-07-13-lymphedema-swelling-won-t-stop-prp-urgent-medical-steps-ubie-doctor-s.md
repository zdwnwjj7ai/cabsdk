Understanding Persistent Swelling in Lymphedema

Lymphedema occurs when lymph fluid builds up in soft tissues. This can be primary (from birth or genetic mutations) or secondary (after surgery, radiation, infection or injury). Reasons swelling won't stop include:

Damaged lymph vesselsSurgery or radiation for cancer can injure nodes and vessels, creating a bottleneck for fluid.

Scar tissue formationRepeated injury or infection leads to fibrosis, making tissues stiff and less able to drain.

Inflammation and infectionCellulitis or recurrent infections worsen fluid build-up and tissue damage.

Obesity and inactivityExcess weight and limited muscle pumping can tip the balance toward fluid retention.

Improper or incomplete therapyCompression without proper fitting, insufficient manual lymphatic drainage (MLD) or lack of skin care can reduce effectiveness.

If you're still swollen despite standard therapies, it's time to look deeper at both diagnosis and advanced treatments.

What Is PRP and Why Consider It for Lymphedema?

Platelet-Rich Plasma (PRP) is a concentration of your own platelets in a small volume of plasma. Platelets release growth factors and cytokines that:

Stimulate new blood vessel formation (angiogenesis)

Encourage tissue regeneration

Modulate inflammation

In orthopedics and dermatology, PRP has shown promise in healing tendons, skin ulcers and joint injuries. The rationale for PRP in lymphedema:

Regenerating lymphatic vesselsEarly lab studies suggest growth factors may foster lymphangiogenesis (new lymph channel growth).

Reducing inflammationPRP's anti-inflammatory properties can ease tissue fibrosis.

Enhancing surgical outcomesWhen combined with lymphaticovenous anastomosis (LVA) or vascularized lymph node transfer (VLNT), PRP might speed healing.

What Does the Research Say?

Research on PRP for lymphedema is still emerging. Key points from credible sources:

Animal studiesRodent models show improved lymphatic regeneration and reduced swelling with PRP injections.

Small human trialsA handful of pilot studies report mild to moderate reduction in limb volume and improved quality of life when PRP is injected into or around lymphatic vessels.

Combination therapiesEarly reports suggest PRP plus microsurgical techniques yields better long-term results than surgery alone.

Limitations to keep in mind:

Small sample sizes

Variable PRP preparation methods

Short follow-up periods (6–12 months only)

Larger, controlled trials are still needed before PRP can be called standard of care. Yet for those who have exhausted conservative measures, PRP may be worth discussing with a specialist.

When to Seek Immediate Medical Attention

While lymphedema itself is rarely life-threatening, complications can be serious:

Sudden leg or arm swelling with pain and redness (possible blood clot)

Fever or chills with worsening swelling (infection/cellulitis)

Worsening shortness of breath or chest pain

If you experience these, seek emergency care. For non-urgent concerns, speak to your doctor promptly.
