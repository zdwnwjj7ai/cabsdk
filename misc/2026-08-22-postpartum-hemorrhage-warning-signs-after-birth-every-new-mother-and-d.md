Postpartum Hemorrhage: Warning Signs After Birth Every New Mother and Doctor Should Know

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) is excessive bleeding after childbirth, defined as blood loss greater than 500 mL after vaginal delivery or 1,000 mL after cesarean. Without prompt treatment, PPH can become life-threatening.

Warning signs of postpartum hemorrhage:

Soaking through a pad every hour for two consecutive hours

Passing large blood clots

Dizziness or lightheadedness

Rapid heart rate

Low blood pressure

A soft or "boggy" uterus

Renewed heavy bleeding days or weeks after delivery (secondary PPH)

PPH can escalate within minutes, and secondary PPH may appear up to 12 weeks postpartum—long after hospital discharge. Recognizing symptoms early can be the difference between a quick intervention and a medical emergency. Because postpartum bleeding patterns vary, it's not always obvious when something has crossed into dangerous territory. What Is Postpartum Hemorrhage?

Postpartum hemorrhage refers to heavy bleeding after vaginal or cesarean birth. It's typically classified as:

Primary PPH: More than 500 mL of blood loss within 24 hours of a vaginal birth or more than 1,000 mL after a cesarean.

Secondary PPH: Excessive bleeding occurring between 24 hours and 12 weeks postpartum.

While PPH affects about 1–5% of new mothers globally, prompt recognition and treatment make it highly manageable.

Why It Happens: Common Risk Factors

Even without known risk factors, any new mother can develop PPH. Still, certain conditions raise the likelihood:

Uterine atony (when the uterus doesn't contract firmly)

Retained placenta or placental fragments

Prolonged labor or very rapid labor

Multiple gestation (twins, triplets)

High blood pressure disorders (preeclampsia)

Use of certain medications during labor (e.g., magnesium sulfate)

Previous postpartum hemorrhage

If you fall into one or more of these categories, your care team may monitor you more closely in the first hours and days after delivery.

Key Warning Signs to Watch For

It's normal to have some bleeding (lochia) for several weeks after birth—first bright red, then pinkish or brownish, finally yellowish-white. But certain changes mean you need immediate medical attention:

Soaking through one or more pads every hour for 2 consecutive hours

Large blood clots (bigger than a golf ball)

Dizziness, lightheadedness, or fainting

Rapid heart rate (over 100 beats per minute)

Low blood pressure (feeling very weak or disoriented)

Extreme fatigue or weakness

When to Seek Immediate Help

If you experience any of the warning signs above, don't wait. Call your obstetrician or midwife, or go to the nearest emergency department. Remember, you are not overreacting—excessive blood loss can become life threatening within minutes.

How Providers Prevent and Manage PPH

Hospitals follow established protocols to reduce and control bleeding:

Administering a uterotonic drug (e.g., oxytocin) right after the baby is born

Gentle controlled traction on the umbilical cord

Immediate massage of the uterus through the abdomen to encourage contraction

Accurate measurement of blood and fluid intake

Regular checks of vital signs and uterine tone

Additional uterotonics (methylergonovine, misoprostol) if bleeding persists

Tranexamic acid to help blood clot

In severe cases, surgical procedures (e.g., uterine balloon tamponade, artery ligation, or hysterectomy)

Keep a daily log of pad changes and note how heavy the bleeding is.

When Secondary PPH Occurs

Bleeding that returns or worsens after the first week—up to 12 weeks postpartum—may signal secondary PPH. Common causes include:

Retained placental tissue

Infection (endometritis)

Subinvolution of the uterus (uterus not returning to normal size)

Warning signs are similar to primary PPH: heavy bleeding, fever, foul-smelling discharge, or pelvic pain. Seek prompt evaluation if these occur.

Postpartum hemorrhage can develop quickly. Early detection and treatment are key.
