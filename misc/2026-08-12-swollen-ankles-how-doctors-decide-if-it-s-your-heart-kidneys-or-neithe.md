Swollen Ankles: How Doctors Decide If It's Your Heart, Kidneys, or Neither

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Swollen ankles and feet can result from heart failure, kidney disease, liver problems, or less serious lifestyle and local causes. Doctors typically identify the underlying cause through a combination of medical history, physical examination, lab tests, and imaging studies.

Key warning signs include sudden swelling, swelling in only one leg, chest pain, shortness of breath, or skin discoloration—all of which may indicate a serious condition requiring urgent care. Most cases, however, have manageable causes such as prolonged standing, high salt intake, pregnancy, or medication side effects.

Because swollen ankles and feet can signal anything from a minor issue to a life-threatening condition, identifying the likely cause early is critical. Explanation

Swollen ankles and feet causes range from harmless to serious. Fluid buildup (edema) often leads to puffiness, stretching of the skin, and tightness in your shoes. While many cases result from lifestyle or local issues, other times the heart, kidneys, or liver may be involved. Here's how doctors work through the most common swollen ankles and feet causes to determine what's behind your swelling—and when to get help.

1. Taking a Detailed Medical History

Your doctor's first step is listening. They'll ask about:

Sudden vs. gradual swelling

Daily pattern (worse by evening?)

One foot or both?

Involvement of legs, hands, or abdomen

Shortness of breath, chest pain, fatigue

Changes in urination, weight gain, abdominal bloating

Blood pressure drugs (e.g., calcium channel blockers)

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)

History of heart disease, kidney problems, liver disease

Recent travel, immobility, or injury

Diet (salt intake)

Exercise habits, alcohol consumption

2. Physical Examination

Next, the doctor checks for clues on exam:

Pitting edema: Pressing skin over the shin or ankle for 5 seconds leaves a dent

Skin changes: Discoloration, thickening, ulcers (venous stasis vs. lymphedema)

Jugular venous pressure (JVP): Elevated in heart failure

Lung sounds: Crackles or wheezes may suggest fluid in the lungs

Abdominal exam: Fluid wave or shifting dullness points to ascites (liver/kidney)

Pulse and blood pressure: Assessing circulatory status

3. Laboratory Tests and Imaging

Depending on history and exam findings, your doctor may order:

B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) or N-terminal proBNP: elevated in heart failure

Serum creatinine and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR): kidney function

Liver function tests (ALT, AST, bilirubin, albumin)

Electrolytes: sodium, potassium (imbalances worsen edema)

Proteinuria or blood: signs of kidney disease

Enlarged heart, fluid in lung fields

Heart chamber size, ejection fraction, valve function

Venous obstruction, deep vein thrombosis

Ascites or liver cirrhosis signs

4. Ruling In or Out Heart-Related Causes

Heart failure leads to fluid backing up into the legs. Key features include:

Breathlessness on exertion or lying flat

Fatigue, rapid weight gain (fluid retention)

Elevated JVP, crackles in lungs

Pitting edema in both ankles and sometimes abdomen

High BNP or NT-proBNP

Echo showing reduced ejection fraction or diastolic dysfunction

If heart disease is suspected, your doctor may refer you to a cardiologist for additional tests such as stress echocardiography, CT coronary angiography, or cardiac MRI.

5. Ruling In or Out Kidney-Related Causes

When kidneys can't remove enough fluid, edema results. Look for:

Decreased urine output, foamy urine (proteinuria)

Fatigue, poor appetite

Generalized swelling—ankles, hands, even around the eyes

Elevated creatinine, low eGFR

Significant protein or blood in the urine

Referral to a nephrologist and tests like a 24-hour urine protein collection or kidney ultrasound may follow.

6. Other Systemic Causes

Even if heart and kidneys check out, other causes can lead to swollen ankles:

Low albumin production → fluid leaks into tissues

Signs: jaundice, spider veins, ascites

Damaged leg veins, varicose veins

Heaviness, itching, skin discoloration

Blocked lymphatic drainage (infection, surgery)

Non-pitting, firm swelling, skin thickening

Calcium channel blockers (e.g., amlodipine)

Steroids, certain diabetes drugs

Sprains, cellulitis

Redness, warmth, tenderness

Pregnancy or premenstrual fluid shifts

Prolonged standing or sitting

High-salt diet

8. Treatment Approaches

Once a cause is identified, treatment may include:

Elevating legs several times a day

Reducing sodium in your diet

Regular exercise and weight management

Diuretics ("water pills") for fluid removal

ACE inhibitors or beta-blockers for heart failure

Adjusting or stopping drugs that worsen edema

Graduated compression stockings for venous insufficiency

Lymphatic drainage massage for lymphedema

Varicose vein ablation or ligation

Regular weight checks, blood tests, and follow-up imaging

9. When to Seek Immediate Medical Attention

While many causes of swollen ankles and feet are manageable, some signs may indicate a serious problem. Call your doctor or go to the emergency department if you have:

Sudden or severe swelling in one leg (could signal deep vein thrombosis)

Chest pain, rapid heartbeat, or difficulty breathing (heart emergency)

10. Key Takeaways

Swollen ankles and feet causes include heart failure, kidney disease, liver issues, venous problems, lymphedema, medications, and local injury.

Doctors use history, exam, lab tests, and imaging to pinpoint the root cause.

Simple lifestyle changes and medications often relieve mild to moderate edema.

Serious symptoms warrant prompt evaluation—don't ignore sudden or painful swelling.
