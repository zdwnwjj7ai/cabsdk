# Soccer Star Kasey Keller Reveals He Played Through His Final Season With Cancer

56-YEAR-OLD KASEY KELLER was all smiles as I apologized for joining our Zoom call a few minutes late. It’s been two decades since I first saw the starting goalkeeper on TV playing in the 2006 FIFA World Cup in Germany. He looked the same, except this time he wasn’t leaping around, making those two glorious saves in the final 20 minutes of the match against Italy. Now, on my computer screen, Kasey Keller was ready to talk to me about something he'd never shared publicly.

We started back in 2010. He had just finished his second season with the Seattle Sounders when he started feeling some hip pain. Keller had signed a two-year contract with the Sounders and didn’t want to spend his final year on the treatment table.

“I just wanted to make sure that the hip wasn't anything serious. That I was just a little bit beat up,” Keller said. “I was almost 41 years old and had 700 matches.” Hip pain made sense for someone with his record.

Keller spent 17 years playing professionally across the US and Europe. This includes the 12 years as starting goalkeeper for the U.S. Men’s National Team, playing in international tournaments like the 1995 Copa America and the 1996 Atlanta Olympics. He also played for Team USA in four World Cup Tournaments, and abroad in some European clubs. In 2009, he joined the Seattle Sounders as both goalkeeper and captain in their early Major League Soccer Era.

He had an MRI scan around midday, and didn’t think much of it, going to get lunch with his wife afterward. At around 2 PM, he got a phone call from the team doctor. The conversation, he says, was unusual. Instead of talking about hip pain, the doctor asked if he'd had lunch.

"I said, 'Doc I've had a lot of MRIs, and nobody's ever asked me if I had lunch afterward.'" They'd found something on the MRI, and wanted him to go back to the hospital for a biopsy—but only if he hadn't eaten. Because he had, the doctor scheduled him for another scan at 6 AM the following morning. It didn’t sound good.

The biopsy results took around four days. Then came the answer: Non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma, a blood cancer. Keller met with an oncologist afterward who told him that it was a slow-moving cancer, and that he was asymptomatic. In fact, they hadn’t found this because of anything cancer-related. The cancer discovery was technically an accident.

The oncologist’s suggestion was to wait and monitor. Keller admits he felt a bit weird about it considering he had one more year with the Seattle Sounders. He didn’t think he could sign a new contract when he may have unpredictable treatment schedules in his future.

In a lot of ways, Keller says the diagnosis was exactly the same. “It was just one more thing that I don't know what's going to happen. So why is it going to affect me until there's a reason for it to affect me? As a pro-player, you learn to compartmentalize those types of things.”

In spite of his cancer diagnosis, Keller finished his last season with the Seattle Sounders with 1.09 goals against average and 18 wins, and was named the 2011 MLS goalkeeper of the year.

A Mysterious Bump on the Groin

After his retirement from professional soccer, Keller worked as an analyst and commentator on ESPN and Seattle Sounders' broadcasts. Ultimately, he didn't need to start treatment until 8 years after his diagnosis. But there were warning signs such as unexplained weight loss.

Keller says he was coming out of the shower one day when his wife took one look at him and told him he needed to see the doctor. There was a noticeable bump on his groin.

One of the symptoms of Non-Hodgkin lymphoma is swollen lymph nodes. These are most often visible in the neck, armpits, and groin. “The lymph nodes in my groin looked like they were going to come through my skin,” Keller recalls.

He went back to the doctor where he started showing more concerning results on blood tests. He started treatment in the beginning of March 2018.

It Got Worse Before It Got Better

Keller went through multiple treatments, finding temporary relief. “We had a little bit of success. We've had a little bit of movement, but it just wasn't what I was expecting,” he says.

The doctor also informed him that his cancer had changed to a more aggressive blood cancer: large B-cell lymphoma. The news made him eligible for a one-time treatment called Breyanzi from Bristol Myers Squibb that became available in 2021. The drug collects and genetically modifies a person’s white blood cells to become CAR T cells that recognize and attack lymphoma.

“I had my T cells spun out of my blood. I had that month where I was taking some orals while they were waiting for the cells to be engineered in the lab and then had the infusion,” Keller says.

He recalls one nurse who told him to prepare for a severe fever that could land him in the hospital for a few days. (The warning label for Breyanzi does include a possible fever of 100.4°F or higher).
