Persistent Ear Pain? Why Your Ear Anatomy Is Hurting & Medically Approved Next Steps

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Persistent ear pain has several common causes, most tied to ear anatomy: outer ear issues like swimmer's ear or earwax buildup, middle ear infections, pressure from a blocked eustachian tube, or referred pain from the jaw, teeth, throat, or neck.

To manage ear pain at home, keep the ear dry, avoid inserting objects into the canal, use over-the-counter pain relievers, and apply prescribed ear drops or wax softeners when appropriate. Seek medical care if pain lasts more than 3 to 5 days. Get urgent care for high fever, foul or bloody drainage, hearing loss, severe headache, dizziness, swelling behind the ear, or facial weakness.

Because ear pain has so many possible causes—ranging from minor to serious—identifying the likely source is the fastest way to know whether to treat at home, book a visit, or seek urgent care. Explanation

Persistent ear pain can be frustrating, distracting, and sometimes worrying. If your ear has been hurting for more than a day or two—or keeps coming back—it's important to understand why. The answer usually lies in your ear anatomy and how different parts of the ear respond to infection, pressure, inflammation, or injury.

Let's break it down in simple, clear terms so you know what might be happening—and what to do next.

Understanding Your Ear Anatomy

Your ear is more complex than it looks. It has three main parts:

1. The Outer Ear

This includes:

The visible part (auricle)

The ear canal

The outer ear collects sound and directs it inward. The ear canal also produces earwax (cerumen), which protects against bacteria and debris.

Common causes of pain here:

Swimmer's ear (outer ear infection)

Trapped water

Earwax buildup

Scratches from cotton swabs or earbuds

Pain in the outer ear often gets worse when you touch or pull on it.

2. The Middle Ear

Located behind the eardrum, the middle ear contains:

Three tiny bones (ossicles)

The eustachian tube (connects the ear to the back of the throat)

The middle ear helps transmit sound vibrations and equalizes pressure.

Middle ear infection (otitis media)

Fluid buildup

Blocked eustachian tube from allergies or colds

Rapid pressure changes (flying, diving)

Middle ear pain often feels deep, throbbing, or associated with pressure and temporary hearing changes.

3. The Inner Ear

The inner ear contains:

The cochlea (hearing)

The vestibular system (balance)

While inner ear problems typically cause dizziness or balance issues rather than pain, inflammation in nearby structures can still create discomfort.

Why Persistent Ear Pain Happens

Ear pain that doesn't go away usually falls into one of two categories:

1. Primary Ear Pain (Problem Inside the Ear)

This is when the issue directly affects your ear anatomy.

Common causes include:

Infection (bacterial or viral)

Fluid behind the eardrum

Ruptured eardrum

Earwax impaction

Chronic swimmer's ear

Eustachian tube dysfunction

Infections are especially common in children but also occur in adults, particularly after a cold or sinus infection.

2. Referred Pain (Pain Coming From Somewhere Else)

Because nerves around the ear are shared with the jaw, throat, teeth, and neck, pain may feel like it's in your ear—even when the ear itself is normal.

Common sources include:

TMJ (jaw joint dysfunction)

Dental infections

Throat infections

Tonsillitis

Sinus infections

Neck muscle strain

If your doctor examines your ear and says it "looks normal," this is often why.

When Persistent Ear Pain Is More Serious

Most ear pain is not life-threatening. However, it should not be ignored—especially if it lasts more than a few days.

Speak to a doctor promptly if you notice:

Fever above 102°F (39°C)

Severe headache

Swelling behind the ear

Facial weakness

Persistent dizziness

Bloody or foul-smelling drainage

Hearing loss that doesn't improve

Pain lasting more than 3–5 days

Pain in someone with diabetes or a weakened immune system

In rare cases, untreated infections can spread beyond the ear. That's why persistent pain deserves attention.

Medically Approved Next Steps

The right treatment depends entirely on which part of your ear anatomy is affected.

For Suspected Outer Ear Infection:

Keep the ear dry

Avoid inserting anything into the canal

Use doctor-prescribed antibiotic or antifungal drops if needed

Over-the-counter pain relievers (as directed)

Do not attempt to clean deep inside the ear.

For Middle Ear Infection:

Many mild cases improve on their own

Pain relievers such as acetaminophen or ibuprofen can help

Antibiotics may be prescribed if bacterial infection is confirmed

Nasal saline or allergy treatment may help relieve pressure

Adults with repeated middle ear infections may need further evaluation of eustachian tube function.

For Earwax Buildup:

Use over-the-counter ear drops designed to soften wax

Consider professional ear cleaning

Avoid cotton swabs (they push wax deeper)

For TMJ or Jaw-Related Pain:

Soft diet temporarily

Warm compress

Stress reduction

Night guard if recommended by a dentist

Physical therapy in some cases

What NOT to Do

To protect your ear anatomy and avoid worsening the issue:

Don't insert cotton swabs, hairpins, or sharp objects

Don't ignore worsening pain

Don't self-prescribe leftover antibiotics

Don't delay evaluation if symptoms are severe

The ear is delicate. Improper cleaning or treatment can cause damage.

How Long Is Too Long?

General guidance:

Mild pain: monitor for 24–48 hours

Pain lasting more than 3 days: see a healthcare professional

Recurrent pain: schedule evaluation

Sudden severe pain: seek urgent care

Chronic ear pain (lasting weeks) should always be evaluated, especially in adults. Persistent symptoms can sometimes signal structural issues or, rarely, more serious underlying conditions.

The Bottom Line

Persistent ear pain usually comes down to something affecting your ear anatomy—either the outer ear, middle ear, or nearby structures like the jaw or throat.

Most causes are treatable and not dangerous. However, ongoing pain is your body's signal that something needs attention.

Start by:

Monitoring your symptoms

Avoiding ear canal trauma

Using a free symptom checker for ear pain to identify possible causes and receive guidance on next steps

Scheduling a medical visit if symptoms persist

And most importantly: Speak to a doctor immediately if you experience severe symptoms, neurological changes, high fever, or worsening pain. Early evaluation can prevent complications and provide relief faster.

Your ears are small—but their anatomy is complex. Treat persistent pain seriously, get accurate information, and don't hesitate to seek professional care when needed.
