Red Dots on Your Skin: What Doctors Say About Cherry Angiomas

Cherry angiomas—sometimes called "senile angiomas" or "Campbell de Morgan spots"—are small, bright red or purple bumps that appear on the skin. They're common, benign (non-cancerous) vascular growths. Although they can look alarming, especially if you've never seen one before, they're almost always harmless.

What Are Cherry Angiomas?

Cherry angiomas are collections of tiny blood vessels (capillaries) that form in the top layers of the skin. They usually:

Measure between 1 – 6 millimeters in diameter.

Range in color from bright red to deep purple.

Occur anywhere on the body but favor the trunk, arms, and shoulders.

Tend to increase in number with age, particularly after age 30.

Most people never notice their first cherry angioma. Over time, though, they may develop more of them.

Cherry Angiomas Causes

The exact cause of cherry angiomas remains unclear, but doctors and researchers point to several factors that seem to play a role:

Age• They become more common as people get older.• Studies show up to 75% of adults over age 75 have at least one.

Genetics• Family history can increase your chances.• Certain inherited traits affect blood vessel growth.

Hormonal Changes• Pregnancy and hormonal therapies may trigger new spots.• Fluctuations in estrogen and progesterone can influence capillary growth.

Climate and Environment• Warm, humid climates might promote the formation of angiomas.• Some research suggests repeated sun exposure could play a minor role.

Chemical Exposure• Long-term contact with certain chemicals—like mustard gas or bromides—has been linked in rare cases.• Occupational exposures are uncommon but documented in older studies.

Underlying Health Conditions• Rarely, liver disease or blood disorders may accompany clusters of angiomas.• Most people with cherry angiomas have no other medical issues.

Recognizing Cherry Angiomas

Cherry angiomas usually stand out because of their color and texture:

They're round or oval, with smooth or slightly raised tops.

They may feel soft or slightly firm when you press on them.

If scratched, pinched, or otherwise irritated, they can bleed briefly because they're made of blood vessels.

These lesions don't hurt or itch under normal circumstances.

When to Seek Medical Advice

Although cherry angiomas are benign, you should consult a healthcare professional if you notice:

Rapid changes in size, shape, or color.

Bleeding that doesn't stop quickly or recurs.

Pain, itching, or tenderness around the bump.

A single spot that becomes unusually large.

How Doctors Diagnose Cherry Angiomas

A dermatologist or primary care provider can usually diagnose a cherry angioma by looking at it under good lighting. They may also use:

Dermoscopy: A handheld magnifying tool that highlights blood vessel patterns.

Skin Biopsy: Rarely needed, but a small tissue sample can rule out other vascular growths or skin cancers.

Treatment Options

Most cherry angiomas require no treatment unless they:

Bother you cosmetically.

Tend to bleed from everyday friction (e.g., rubbing on clothing).

Are located in areas prone to repeated trauma.

Common treatment methods include:

Electrocautery• A small electric current burns the angioma.• Blood vessels seal off as the tissue heals.

Laser Therapy• Pulsed-dye lasers target hemoglobin in the blood vessels.• Minimizes bleeding and scarring.

Cryotherapy• Liquid nitrogen freezes the angioma, causing it to fall off.• Faster recovery but slightly more risk of temporary skin discoloration.

Shave Excision• The doctor shaves off the bump with a small blade.• Local anesthesia ensures comfort.

Each method has pros and cons. Your dermatologist can recommend the best choice based on the size, location, and number of angiomas, as well as your skin type.

Preventing Cherry Angiomas

Because the root causes aren't fully understood, there's no guaranteed way to prevent cherry angiomas. However, you can take general skin-health steps:

Protect your skin from excessive sun exposure with broad-spectrum SPF and sun-protective clothing.

Stay calm: These spots are very common and generally harmless.

Monitor changes: Keep a photo log or note the date when new spots appear.

Protect vulnerable areas: If an angioma is in an area that catches on jewelry or clothing, consider treatment.

When Something Might Be More Serious

While cherry angiomas aren't cancerous, other red or dark spots on your skin could be. Be alert for:

Asymmetrical borders.

Multiple colors within one spot.

Rapid growth.
