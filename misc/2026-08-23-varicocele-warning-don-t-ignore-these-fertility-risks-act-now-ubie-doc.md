Do You Have a Varicocele? Symptoms, Fertility Risks, and Your Next Steps

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

A varicocele is an enlargement of the scrotal veins that can cause a dull ache, scrotal heaviness, or a "bag of worms" sensation. While many men experience no symptoms, a varicocele may impair sperm quality, reduce testosterone, and contribute to male infertility.

Diagnosis typically involves a physical exam and scrotal ultrasound. Treatment ranges from supportive care to surgical repair or embolization, depending on pain level, testicular changes, and fertility goals. Sudden, severe scrotal pain requires urgent medical attention, and several other red flags should guide your next steps.

Because varicocele symptoms overlap with other scrotal conditions—some of which are serious—it's important to clarify what's actually happening before deciding on next steps. Explanation

A varicocele is a common condition that affects the veins inside the scrotum (the sac that holds the testicles). It happens when these veins become enlarged, similar to varicose veins in the legs.

Many men with a varicocele don't realize they have one. Others notice discomfort, swelling, or fertility problems. Understanding varicocele and infertility signs can help you decide when to monitor symptoms and when to seek medical care.

This guide explains what a varicocele is, the symptoms to watch for, how it can affect fertility, and what you can do next.

What Is a Varicocele?

Inside the scrotum is a network of veins called the pampiniform plexus. These veins help regulate the temperature of the testicles, which is important for sperm production.

Veins become enlarged due to poor blood flow.

Valves inside the veins do not work properly.

Blood pools instead of circulating efficiently.

Varicoceles are common. Medical studies estimate they affect:

About 15% of all men

Up to 40% of men evaluated for infertility

They most often occur on the left side, due to differences in how veins drain on that side of the body.

Common Symptoms of Varicocele

Not everyone with a varicocele has symptoms. In fact, many cases are discovered during fertility testing or a routine physical exam.

When symptoms do occur, they may include:

A dull, aching pain in the scrotum

A feeling of heaviness or dragging in the testicle

Visible or enlarged veins that may look like a "bag of worms"

Scrotal swelling

Pain that worsens after standing for long periods or physical activity

Relief when lying down

The discomfort is usually mild but can become more noticeable over time.

Varicocele and Infertility Signs

One of the most important concerns with varicoceles is their link to fertility problems.

Signs of Varicocele-Related Infertility

You usually cannot "feel" infertility. However, possible varicocele and infertility signs include:

Difficulty conceiving after 12 months of regular, unprotected intercourse

Abnormal semen analysis results (low sperm count, poor motility, abnormal shape)

Shrinking (atrophy) of one testicle, especially in adolescents

Low testosterone symptoms such as reduced energy or libido

It's important to note that not every varicocele causes infertility. Many men with varicoceles have normal sperm counts and father children without difficulty.

When Should You Be Concerned?

A varicocele is usually not life-threatening. However, you should pay closer attention if you experience:

Persistent or worsening scrotal pain

Noticeable testicular shrinkage

Fertility challenges

A sudden new mass in the scrotum

Severe pain that comes on quickly

Sudden, intense scrotal pain is not typical for a simple varicocele and requires immediate medical evaluation to rule out emergencies such as testicular torsion.

How Is a Varicocele Diagnosed?

A doctor—usually a primary care provider or urologist—can diagnose a varicocele through:

1. Physical Exam

Examining the scrotum while you are standing

Asking you to bear down (Valsalva maneuver) to make the veins more visible

2. Scrotal Ultrasound

Uses sound waves to confirm enlarged veins

Measures blood flow and vein size

Helps rule out other causes of swelling

3. Semen Analysis (if fertility is a concern)

Evaluates sperm count, movement, and shape

Often repeated to confirm results

Early diagnosis is especially important for adolescents, as untreated varicoceles may affect testicular development.

Do All Varicoceles Need Treatment?

No. Treatment depends on symptoms and fertility goals.

You may not need treatment if:

You have no pain

Your fertility is not affected

Testicular size is normal

Hormone levels are stable

In these cases, monitoring may be enough.

Treatment Options

If a varicocele causes pain, infertility, or testicular shrinkage, treatment may be recommended.

1. Conservative Management

For mild discomfort:

Wear supportive underwear

Avoid prolonged standing

Use over-the-counter pain relief as advised by a doctor

2. Surgical Repair (Varicocelectomy)

This is a common and generally safe procedure where:

The enlarged veins are tied off to redirect blood flow

It is usually done as outpatient surgery

Recovery typically takes 1–2 weeks

Studies show that surgery can improve semen parameters in many men, though pregnancy outcomes vary.

3. Percutaneous Embolization

A minimally invasive procedure where:

A radiologist blocks off affected veins using coils or a special solution

No large incision is required

Recovery is often quicker than surgery

Your doctor will help determine which option is appropriate based on your symptoms, age, and fertility goals.

Can You Prevent a Varicocele?

There is no proven way to prevent a varicocele. They are largely due to vein anatomy and valve function.

However, maintaining overall health supports reproductive function:

Maintain a healthy weight

Avoid tobacco use

Limit excessive heat exposure (e.g., hot tubs)

Manage chronic conditions like diabetes

Your Next Steps

If you suspect a varicocele, consider the following:

Monitor symptoms (pain, swelling, changes in testicle size)

Seek medical advice if fertility is a goal and conception is delayed

Get a professional exam for persistent discomfort
