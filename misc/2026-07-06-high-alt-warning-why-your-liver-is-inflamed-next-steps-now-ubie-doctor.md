Is Your ALT High? Why Your Liver Is Inflamed & Medically Approved Next Steps

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Elevated ALT levels most often indicate liver inflammation. The most common causes are fatty liver disease, alcohol use, medications or supplements, and viral hepatitis. Less common causes include muscle injury, autoimmune disorders, and genetic conditions.

Recommended next steps include repeating the ALT test, avoiding alcohol, reviewing all medications and supplements with your clinician, screening for hepatitis, considering a liver ultrasound, and making weight, diet, and exercise changes. Seek urgent medical care if you experience jaundice, confusion, severe abdominal pain, vomiting blood, or abdominal swelling.

Explanation

If your ALT blood test came back high, you're probably wondering what it means — and how serious it is.

ALT stands for alanine aminotransferase. It's an enzyme found mostly in your liver. When liver cells are irritated, inflamed, or damaged, ALT can leak into the bloodstream. That's why doctors use the ALT blood test as a key marker of liver health.

An elevated ALT does not automatically mean something severe is happening. But it does mean your liver may be under stress — and it deserves attention.

Let's break down what this means, what causes it, and what you should do next.

What Is a Normal ALT Level?

Normal ranges vary slightly by lab, but generally:

Men: About 7–55 units per liter (U/L)

Women: About 7–45 U/L

Some experts now suggest that the upper limit should be even lower, particularly for women, because mild elevations can still signal early liver disease.

If your ALT is just slightly elevated, your doctor may repeat the ALT blood test in a few weeks to confirm the result. Temporary increases are common.

If it's significantly elevated (for example, 2–3 times the normal range or higher), further evaluation is usually recommended.

Why Is Your ALT High?

ALT rises when liver cells are inflamed or injured. The causes range from common and reversible to more serious conditions.

1. Fatty Liver Disease (Very Common)

The most common reason for elevated ALT in the U.S. and many other countries is nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD).

This happens when excess fat builds up in the liver. Risk factors include:

Overweight or obesity

Type 2 diabetes

High cholesterol

High triglycerides

Insulin resistance

Metabolic syndrome

Many people with fatty liver have no symptoms at all. Often, a routine ALT blood test is the first clue.

The good news: early fatty liver can often be reversed with lifestyle changes.

2. Alcohol-Related Liver Inflammation

Drinking alcohol — especially regularly or heavily — can raise ALT levels.

Even moderate alcohol use may elevate ALT in some people. Over time, alcohol can cause:

Alcoholic fatty liver

Alcoholic hepatitis

Cirrhosis

If alcohol is contributing, reducing or eliminating alcohol intake often improves ALT levels.

3. Viral Hepatitis

Chronic infections such as:

Hepatitis B

Hepatitis C

can cause persistent ALT elevation.

These infections may not cause obvious symptoms early on. That's why blood testing is critical. If you're concerned about persistent liver inflammation or experiencing unexplained symptoms, you can check if your symptoms align with chronic hepatitis using a free AI-powered symptom assessment tool before your next doctor's appointment.

Early detection is important because treatment can prevent long-term liver damage.

4. Medication or Supplement Effects

Many medications can elevate ALT, including:

Acetaminophen (especially in high doses)

Certain antibiotics

Statins

Anti-seizure medications

Some herbal supplements

"Natural" does not always mean safe for the liver. Always tell your doctor about every medication and supplement you take.

5. Muscle Injury or Intense Exercise

ALT is mostly found in the liver, but small amounts are present in muscle tissue. Very intense exercise or muscle injury can mildly raise ALT.

Your doctor may check other labs (like AST or CK) to help sort this out.

6. Autoimmune or Genetic Conditions

Less common causes include:

Autoimmune hepatitis

Hemochromatosis (iron overload)

Wilson's disease (copper buildup)

Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency

These conditions are uncommon but important to identify early.

How Serious Is a High ALT?

The seriousness depends on:

How high the level is

How long it's been elevated

Your overall health and risk factors

Other abnormal liver test results

A mildly elevated ALT (for example, 60–80 U/L) is often related to fatty liver or temporary inflammation.

Very high levels (for example, 10 times the upper limit of normal) may indicate acute liver injury and require urgent medical attention.

The key point: ALT is a signal, not a diagnosis.

Symptoms of Liver Inflammation

Many people with elevated ALT have no symptoms.

When symptoms do occur, they may include:

Fatigue

Mild abdominal discomfort (right upper side)

Nausea

Loss of appetite

Yellowing of the skin or eyes (jaundice)

Dark urine

Pale stools

If you notice jaundice, confusion, severe abdominal pain, vomiting blood, or swelling in your abdomen, seek immediate medical care.

Medically Approved Next Steps

If your ALT blood test is high, here's what doctors typically recommend.

1. Repeat the ALT Blood Test

Sometimes elevations are temporary. Your provider may repeat the test in:

2–4 weeks (if mildly elevated)

Sooner if levels are very high

Avoid alcohol and unnecessary medications before retesting.

2. Review Medications and Supplements

Your doctor may:

Adjust or stop certain medications

Recommend safer alternatives

Ask about over-the-counter pain relievers

Never stop prescription medication without medical advice.

are often part of the workup, especially if ALT remains elevated.

4. Imaging Tests

An ultrasound of the liver may be ordered to check for:

Fatty liver

Liver enlargement

Structural abnormalities

Ultrasound is painless and noninvasive.

5. Lifestyle Changes (If Fatty Liver Is Suspected)

Evidence-based steps that improve liver health include:

Losing 5–10% of body weight (if overweight)

Eating a Mediterranean-style diet

Reducing added sugars and refined carbs

Limiting alcohol

Exercising at least 150 minutes per week

Managing blood sugar and cholesterol

Even modest weight loss can significantly lower ALT levels.

Can ALT Levels Go Back to Normal?

Yes — often they can.

If the cause is:

Fatty liver → lifestyle changes can normalize ALT

Alcohol use → stopping alcohol may improve levels

Medication-related → stopping the offending drug often helps

Viral hepatitis → treatment can reduce inflammation

The liver has a remarkable ability to heal — especially when problems are caught early.

When Should You Be Concerned?

You should speak to a doctor promptly if:

ALT is more than 2–3 times normal

Levels continue rising

If you ever experience confusion, severe weakness, yellowing of the eyes, or significant abdominal swelling, seek urgent care. Some liver conditions can become life-threatening if untreated.

The Bottom Line

A high ALT blood test result means your liver may be inflamed — but it does not automatically mean something catastrophic.

Common causes include:

Fatty liver disease

Alcohol use

Viral hepatitis

Medication effects

In many cases, the problem is manageable and reversible — especially when addressed early.

The most important next step is simple: speak to a doctor. Only a medical professional can interpret your ALT level in context and decide what testing or treatment is appropriate.

Your liver works hard for you every day. If your ALT is elevated, consider it a signal — not a sentence. With proper evaluation and medically guided action, many people restore their liver health and prevent long-term complications.
