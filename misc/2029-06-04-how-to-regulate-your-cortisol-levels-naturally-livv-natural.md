Have you heard of the “stress hormone?” Otherwise known as cortisol, this hormone plays a major role in how your body responds to stress.

Think of cortisol as your built-in alarm system. It boosts your energy, regulates metabolism, and fine-tunes your immune response.

But there’s a plot twist. Chronic stress can turn this helpful hormone into a source of trouble when your cortisol levels are too high for too long.

Understanding cortisol

Cortisol is a steroid hormone in the glucocorticoid category that’s synthesized from cholesterol. The adrenal glands, found at the head of your kidneys, are the primary producers of cortisol.

This essential hormone affects almost all your tissues and organs, as many contain glucocorticoid receptors.

It can impact your nervous system, respiratory system, immune system, musculoskeletal system, cardiovascular system, integumentary system, and reproductive system.

Below are some of the main roles cortisol plays in your body.

It regulates your body’s stress response.

It controls how your body uses proteins, carbs, fats and plays a role in metabolism.

It suppresses inflammation.

It regulates blood sugar and blood pressure.

It maintains your sleep cycle.

Although it affects various bodily functions, cortisol mainly regulates how your body responds to stress, making it known as the “stress hormone.”

When you experience a stressful event, your body releases cortisol to initiate the “fight or flight” response; a normal reaction to a possible threat.

Your cortisol levels typically spike in the early morning and decline throughout the day, reaching the lowest point around midnight. Night owls may experience an altered pattern due to different sleeping routines.

Below are the normal cortisol measurements.

6 am to 8 am: 10–20 micrograms per deciliter (mcg/dL)

Around 4 pm: 3–10 micrograms per deciliter (mcg/dL)

The hypothalamus and pituitary gland are responsible for regulating the production of cortisol.

When the levels fall below normal, your hypothalamus releases corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), which alerts your pituitary gland to produce adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH). In turn, this interaction stimulates the adrenal glands to produce cortisol.

Optimal cortisol levels are determined by the proper functioning of your pituitary gland, hypothalamus, and adrenal glands.

Unnaturally high or low levels can have a negative impact on your health. As a result, your body keeps an eye on your cortisol levels at all times to ensure homeostasis is maintained.

Chronic stress can lead to elevated levels of cortisol. Prolonged exposure may result in hypercortisolism or Cushing’s syndrome. Here are some other adverse effects of high cortisol levels.

Suppressed immune system

Disrupted sleep-wake cycle

Impaired cognitive function and memory

Increased blood pressure

Disrupted metabolic processes

Adrenal fatigue

Mental health issues, like anxiety and depression

A higher risk of chronic diseases

Individual responses to stress are different, and not everyone experiences the same impact on their cortisol levels. Various factors influence how your body reacts to stress.

Identifying underlying causes

It’s normal for your cortisol levels to increase under acute stress. The problem arises when you experience chronic and sustained elevation.

Increased cortisol levels can occur due to various factors, including lifestyle choices, environmental influences, and psychological contributors. Below are some potential triggers for elevated cortisol levels.

Chronic stress: Whether physical or psychological, chronic stress can cause sustained high cortisol levels. Examples include work-related stress, relationship problems, financial difficulties, and ongoing health problems.

Lack of sleep: Cortisol follows a circadian rhythm, with levels peaking in the early morning to help you wake up. A disruption to this rhythm can lead to high cortisol levels. Examples include insomnia, irregular sleep patterns, and sleeping disorders.

Caffeine intake: Caffeine stimulates the release of cortisol and adrenaline, temporarily increasing alertness. Excess amounts or afternoon/evening consumption can affect cortisol regulation.

Intense exercise: While moderate physical activity helps regulate cortisol, overtraining or excessive endurance exercise can elevate cortisol, especially if the body doesn’t have enough time to rest.

Poor nutrition: High sugar diets, processed foods, and nutrient deficiencies can contribute to high cortisol levels.

Dehydration: A lack of water can activate the stress response and increase your cortisol levels. Proper hydration is vital for stress management.

Medical conditions: Chronic inflammation stimulates the release of cortisol. Autoimmune disorders may cause elevated cortisol levels, while some conditions result in dysregulated cortisol production. Certain medications can also influence this hormone.

Shift work: Working irregular hours can impact cortisol secretion due to a disruption in the natural circadian rhythm.

Nutritional strategies for cortisol balance

Your nutrition plays a crucial role in maintaining balanced hormone levels. Apart from providing your body with essential vitamins and minerals, a balanced diet promotes stable blood sugar levels and prevents deficiencies.

The goal is to give your body the necessary nutrients to regulate cortisol production and maintain your overall health.

Foods that support adrenal health and cortisol regulation

Aim to include a variety of foods in your diet, including fruits, vegetables, whole grains, lean proteins, and healthy fats. Maintain a balanced ratio of carbs, proteins, and fats in every meal to stabilize your blood sugar levels.

Limit your caffeine intake, as excess amounts can result in cortisol spikes. Herbal teas are a brilliant alternative. Reduce your consumption of sugar, as it may lead to insulin spikes.

Here are some foods you should include in your diet to support your adrenal health and regulate cortisol.

Leafy greens like kale and spinach are rich in minerals and vitamins and provide essential nutrients like magnesium.

Berries like strawberries and blueberries are packed with antioxidants, which may ease oxidative stress.

Fatty fish like salmon and mackerel contain omega-3 fatty acids with anti-inflammatory properties, aiding in stress reduction.

Lean proteins like tofu, poultry, and legumes provide the necessary amino acids for the synthesis of neurotransmitters involved in stress responses.

Nuts and seeds, including almonds, walnuts, and flaxseeds, contain nutrients like magnesium and omega-3 fatty acids that contribute to overall adrenal health.

Complex carbohydrates, like quinoa and oats, help stabilize your blood sugar levels, preventing spikes and crashes that trigger cortisol release.

Probiotic-rich foods, like yogurt, kefir, and fermented foods, support your gut health.

Lifestyle modifications

Although nutritional strategies are valuable in the fight against elevated cortisol, they work best in combination with lifestyle modifications and stress management techniques.

Stress reduction techniques and mindfulness practices

It’s vital to manage your stress levels to ensure cortisol regulation. Luckily, there are plenty of effective methods you can choose from. These practices modulate your body’s response to stress and promote a sense of calmness.

Deep breathing exercises: Engage in deep, diaphragmatic breathing to activate your body’s relaxation response and reduce your cortisol levels.

Mindfulness meditation: Practice mindfulness to increase your awareness in the present moment, fostering a calm mind and reducing stress.

Yoga: Combine breath control, meditation, and physical stances to alleviate stress and regulate cortisol.

Progressive muscle relaxation: Tense and relax different muscle groups to release bodily tension and reduce cortisol.

Guided imagery: Close your eyes and imagine a peaceful scene, like a beach or a forest. Engaging all your senses can promote relaxation and lower stress.

Mindful walking: Go for a walk and pay attention to each step, the sensation of your feet on the ground, and your surroundings.

Digital detox: Take regular breaks from your electronic devices to alleviate stress.

Apart from these techniques, you should dedicate time to hobbies and activities that bring you joy and help you relax. It’s also important to schedule regular downtime from work and other responsibilities to prevent burnout.

Regular exercise routine

Physical activity offers numerous benefits for your mind and body. Regular exercise is the best way to regulate cortisol and manage stress naturally. Here are some ways to incorporate movement into your routine.

Aerobic exercise: Activities like swimming, running, cycling, and brisk walking promote a positive mood and reduce cortisol levels. Aim for at least 2.5 hours of moderate-intensity exercise weekly.

Strength training: Resistance exercises, like weightlifting, contribute to cortisol regulation and stress reduction. Introduce strength training activities into your routine at least twice a week.

Interval training: High-intensity interval training (HIIT) involves short bursts of intense exercise followed by periods of rest. It’s associated with improved cortisol levels and better stress response. Incorporate HIIT sessions 1–2 times per week.

Proper sleep hygiene

The term “sleep hygiene” refers to habits that promote good sleep quality and overall sleep health. Adequate rest is crucial for various psychological functions, including cortisol regulation.

Here’s how your sleep hygiene can impact this hormone.

Circadian rhythm alignment: Cortisol follows a diurnal rhythm, so levels typically peak in the early morning and gradually decrease throughout the day. Proper sleep hygiene aligns your sleep-wake cycle with this natural rhythm.

Consistent sleep schedule: Going to bed and waking up at the same time daily keeps your cortisol levels balanced and reinforces your body’s internal clock.

Dark and cool sleeping environment: Darkness tells your body to produce melatonin, a hormone that regulates your sleep-wake cycle. A cool and dark room supports its production and influences cortisol regulation.

Limited exposure to artificial light: The blue light emitted by electronic devices can suppress melatonin production and disrupt your natural circadian rhythm. Minimize screen time before bed to enhance your sleep quality and manage cortisol levels.

Adaptogenic herbs and natural remedies

Adaptogenic herbs are a group of plants that have been used in traditional medicine for centuries. Many believe these herbs help the body adapt to stressors, restore balance, and promote overall well-being.

Rhodiola rosea is shown to enhance the body’s resistance to stress, which helps regulate cortisol levels. It also promotes mental clarity and energy.

Holy basil (Tulsi) may help the body adapt to stressful situations. It delivers calming effects and supports balanced cortisol levels.

Reishi mushroom boasts powerful immune-boosting properties. It also helps the body adapt to stress, potentially regulating cortisol levels indirectly.

Adaptogenic blends combine various herbs to create a synergistic effect for stress management. Popular blends include a mix of ashwagandha, rhodiola, and holy basil.
